01 · What NLP is

The definition

Natural Language Processing is the subfield of computer science and artificial intelligence concerned with getting machines to read, understand and generate human language — English, Hindi, Bengali — as opposed to formal or programming languages.

The evolution in one line

Hand-written rules → statistical models (HMM, CRF) → RNN / LSTM → Transformers (2017, Attention Is All You Need) → BERT, GPT and today's large language models.

01 · What NLP is

Why it is hard

Human language is ambiguous, context-dependent and unstructured.

One sentence, several readings

"I saw a man on a hill with a telescope"

And on the other side

Computers can only compute over numbers. Text must become a numeric representation before any model can touch it.

The entire classic pipeline exists to bridge those two facts.

02 · Why a pipeline exists

A computer cannot do arithmetic on "teaching"

Models take fixed-length numeric vectors. The pipeline is a funnel — every stage cuts noise and vocabulary.

02 · Why a pipeline exists

The running example

"Satyaki was teaching NLP classes at Classroom Tech in Bengaluru, and the students weren't loving it."

Every stage from here on transforms this one sentence, step by step, until it is a vector of numbers.

Vocabulary size is the thing being managed. In Bag-of-Words every unique word is a column. Cutting 50,000 words to 12,000 shrinks the matrix 4×, trains faster, overfits less.

03 · Normalization

The unnamed step 0

Four cleaning operations — each with a trap attached.

Lowercasing

"NLP" and "nlp" should be one token. But US (country) vs us (pronoun), Apple vs apple — which is exactly why NER runs before lowercasing.

Removing HTML, URLs, emoji, punctuation

Task-dependent. For sentiment analysis !!! and emoji are signal, not noise. Do not blindly strip.

Expanding contractions

weren't → were not, it's → it is. Keeps the negation visible instead of hiding it inside a clitic.

Unicode normalization

café has two encodings (é as one codepoint, or e + combining accent). unicodedata.normalize('NFKC', text) resolves it.

04 · Tokenization

A separate task: sentence tokenization

Splitting text into sentences is its own hard problem, because . is ambiguous: Dr., 3.14, etc. NLTK's punkt uses a trained statistical model, not a regex.

from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize
word_tokenize("Dr. Das earned $3.5M. He's happy!")
# ['Dr.', 'Das', 'earned', '$', '3.5M', '.', 'He', "'s", 'happy', '!']

05 · Stemming

Two failure modes to name in an exam

Over-stemming

Two different words collapse to the same stem. Meaning is lost.

university → univers ← universe

Under-stemming

Two related words fail to collapse. The relationship is missed.

ran stays ran, never meets run

Variants: Porter (mild, classic) · Snowball / Porter2 (improved, multilingual) · Lancaster (very aggressive, mangles words).

05 · Lemmatization

Dictionary-based and grammar-aware

Looks up the lemma in WordNet — using the POS tag.

WordPOS suppliedLemma
ranverbrun ✔
betteradjectivegood ✔
meetingnounmeeting
meetingverbmeet

That last pair is the whole point: lemmatization needs POS tagging to work. Without a tag, WordNet defaults to noun and silently under-performs.

lem.lemmatize("teaching")
# 'teaching'  ← defaults to noun, wrong

lem.lemmatize("teaching", pos="v")
# 'teach'     ← correct

06 · Stopwords — stage 3

High-frequency, low-information words

the, is, at, which, on, and, a, an, in — NLTK's English list holds ~179, spaCy's ~326.

~179NLTK list
~326spaCy list
35–45%of tokens removed

The justification is information-theoretic: the appears in essentially every document, so its presence discriminates nothing.

When removing them is correct

Topic classification · document clustering · keyword extraction · search indexing · TF-IDF pipelines.

06 · Stopwords

The practical rule

Stopword lists are a starting point, not gospel. Customize them.

from nltk.corpus import stopwords
sw = set(stopwords.words('english')) - {'not', 'no', 'nor', "n't", 'against'}

Remove from the list

not, no, never, n't for anything sentiment-related.

Add to the list

Domain junk — patient, study in a medical corpus where every document has them.

07 · POS tagging

Why it is hard — ambiguity

11%of word types are ambiguous
~40%of tokens in running text
"They can fish."     can = modal verb (VB)      / fish = verb
"They can fish."     can = to put in a can (VB) / fish = noun
"He saw her duck."   duck = noun or verb?

Resolving this needs context — which is why POS tagging is a sequence labeling problem, not a per-word dictionary lookup.

07 · POS tagging

How it is done, historically

1 · Rule-based

Brill tagger — assign the most frequent tag, then apply transformation rules.

2 · Statistical — HMM

Maximize P(tags | words) from transition P(tagi|tagi-1) and emission P(word|tag), decoded with Viterbi. The textbook answer.

3 · CRF

Conditional Random Fields — discriminative, allows rich overlapping features.

4 · Neural

BiLSTM or Transformer — ~97–98% accuracy today.

08a · Parsing — stage 5

Constituency parsing — phrases inside phrases

Context-Free Grammar. Algorithms: CYK, Earley, probabilistic CFG.

08b · Parsing

Dependency parsing — the modern default

A directed tree: every word points to its syntactic head. No phrase nodes.

08 · Parsing

Why you would actually use it

Relation extraction

"The battery life is terrible but the camera is amazing."

Bag-of-words sees terrible and amazing and shrugs. Dependency parsing yields amod(battery life, terrible) and amod(camera, amazing) — aspect-based sentiment analysis.

The hard problem — attachment ambiguity

"I saw the man with the telescope."

→ telescope attaches to "saw" (I used it)
→ telescope attaches to "man" (he had it)

Both are grammatically valid. Grammar alone cannot decide.

09 · NER

Why it is genuinely hard

Ambiguity

Washington = person, city or state? Apple = fruit or company? Jordan = person or country?

Nested entities

[Bank of [India]GPE]ORG

Novel entities

New startups and people appear daily — a gazetteer can never keep up. The model must generalize from context: "… joined X as CEO" implies X is an organization.

Approaches over time

gazetteer → HMM/CRF with hand-built features (capitalization, prefix/suffix) → BiLSTM-CRF → fine-tuned BERT (~93 F1 on CoNLL-2003)

Real uses

Resume parsing · PII redaction in medical and legal records · knowledge graphs · financial news monitoring · chatbot slot-filling: "book a flight to Delhi on Friday" → DEST=Delhi, DATE=Friday

10a · Vectorization — stage 7

One-hot encoding — the baseline that fails

A 10,000-word vocabulary means each word is a 10,000-dimensional vector of zeros with a single 1.

Enormous and sparse

One dimension per vocabulary entry, and all but one of them is zero.

Every pair is equidistant

cat is exactly as far from dog as it is from democracy. No notion of similarity at all.

10b · Bag of Words

Four limitations

1 · Word order is lost

"dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce identical vectors.

2 · Sparsity

A 50k vocabulary with a 20-word document is 99.96% zeros.

3 · No semantics

good and great are unrelated dimensions.

4 · Frequent words dominate

Which is exactly what TF-IDF fixes.

CountVectorizer(ngram_range=(1,2))
# unigrams + bigrams:  "not", "good", "not good"

Partial fix — n-grams recover local order and rescue negation. Cost: the vocabulary explodes combinatorially.

10c · TF-IDF

Often in this document, rare across the corpus

TermAppears inIDF = log(N / df)Verdict
the10,000 docslog(1) = 0killed entirely
data1,000 docslog(10) ≈ 2.30moderately useful
Bengaluru10 docslog(1000) ≈ 6.91highly distinctive

N = 10,000 documents. TF-IDF automatically down-weights stopwords — explicit removal is often redundant here.

10c · TF-IDF

TF-IDF + Logistic Regression lands within a few points of BERT at roughly a thousandth of the computational cost.

Still a genuinely strong baseline
10d · Word embeddings

Dense vectors where distance means meaning

king   = [ 0.52, -0.18,  0.91, ...,  0.33]   ← 300 numbers
queen  = [ 0.49, -0.21,  0.88, ...,  0.31]   ← very close to king
banana = [-0.72,  0.63, -0.11, ...,  0.90]   ← far away

The distributional hypothesis — Firth, 1957

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps."

Words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. Every embedding method implements this one idea.

10d · Word embeddings

Word2Vec and GloVe

Word2Vec — Mikolov, Google, 2013

A shallow network trained on a fake task; the output layer is thrown away and the hidden weights are the embeddings.

PredictsBetter for
CBOWcentre word ← contextfrequent words, faster
Skip-gramcontext ← centre wordrare words, small corpora
window = 2, centre = "brown"
CBOW:      [the, quick, fox, jumps] → brown
Skip-gram: brown → [the, quick, fox, jumps]

GloVe — Stanford, 2014

Builds a global word–word co-occurrence matrix over the whole corpus and factorizes it, fitting wiTwj + bi + bj ≈ log(Xij).

Word2Vec is predictive and local. GloVe is count-based and global. In practice performance is comparable.

Negative sampling

Instead of a 50,000-way softmax, discriminate the true context word from a handful of random ones. That trick made Word2Vec practical.

10d · The limitation that ended the era

Static embeddings cannot handle polysemy

"I sat on the river bank" → same vector for bank
"I withdrew cash from the bank" → same vector for bank

One vector, averaging the river sense and the money sense into an incoherent blur.

FastText patches OOV by summing character n-grams, so unhappiness composes even if unseen. The polysemy problem stands.

Why this matters historically

BERT produces contextual embeddings — the vector for bank is computed from the sentence it sits in. That single change ended the classical pipeline era.

11 · Ordering

Four rules people get wrong

POS before lemmatization

Otherwise the lemmatizer guesses "noun" and quietly fails.

NER before lowercasing

Capitalization is NER's strongest feature. Lowercase first and you have thrown it away.

Stopword removal after parsing

A parser needs the, of, in to build a tree.

Lemmatize, then remove stopwords

So that inflected forms of stopwords are caught too.

11 · Ordering

What to skip, by task

TaskSkip
Sentiment analysisStopword removal — or at minimum keep the negations
Translation / summarizationStopwords and stemming — you need real, complete words
Feeding a transformerEverything above. BERT and GPT want raw text; their own subword tokenizer handles it
Search indexingNothing — the full stack applies

The big picture

For LLMs this entire pipeline collapses into one step — subword tokenization — and the model learns morphology, syntax and entities implicitly. The classic stack still matters: interpretable, fast, no GPU, works on small data, and it is the standard interview and examination material.

12 · Code

The pipeline in spaCy — part 1

Tokenization, POS tagging, lemmatization and NER all come out of one pass.

import spacy, nltk
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")
doc = nlp("Satyaki was teaching NLP classes at Classroom Tech in Bengaluru.")

# 1. Tokenization    4. POS tagging    2. Lemmatization
for t in doc:
    print(f"{t.text:12} {t.pos_:6} {t.lemma_:12} {t.dep_}")

# 6. NER  (before lowercasing!)
print([(e.text, e.label_) for e in doc.ents])
# [('Satyaki','PERSON'), ('NLP','ORG'), ('Classroom Tech','ORG'), ('Bengaluru','GPE')]
