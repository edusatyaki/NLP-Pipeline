01

What is NLP?

In plain words

Computers are brilliant with numbers and helpless with sentences. NLP is the part of AI that teaches them to deal with the way people actually write and speak.

Every example on this page — is this review happy or angry, who is named in this paragraph, what does this mean in Bengali — is the same problem underneath: language in, decision out.

Natural Language Processing is the subfield of computer science and artificial intelligence concerned with getting machines to read, understand and generate human language — English, Hindi, Bengali — as opposed to formal or programming languages.

Why it is hard

Human language is ambiguous, context-dependent and unstructured. The sentence "I saw a man on a hill with a telescope" has several grammatically valid readings. Meanwhile computers can only compute over numbers — so text must be converted into a numeric representation before any model can touch it. The entire classic pipeline exists to bridge those two facts.

The evolution in one line

Hand-written rules → statistical models (HMM, CRF) → RNN/LSTM → Transformers (2017, Attention Is All You Need) → BERT, GPT and today's large language models.

Common tasks

TaskExample
Text classificationspam vs. not spam
Sentiment analysisproduct review → positive / negative
Machine translationEnglish → Bengali
Named Entity Recognition"Satyaki works at X" → Person, Organization
Summarizationarticle → three lines
Question answeringsearch engines, chatbots
Speech-to-textvoice assistants

The one-line answer for an interview

NLP is the branch of AI that bridges human language and machine understanding — converting unstructured text into structured representations a computer can process, and generating human-readable language back.

02

Why a pipeline exists at all

In plain words

A calculator cannot add up the word teaching. So before any learning can happen, the sentence has to become numbers.

It is done in small steps, like a factory line. Each step throws away something the machine does not need, so the final list of numbers is short enough to work with.

A computer cannot do arithmetic on the string "teaching". Machine learning models take fixed-length numeric vectors as input. The classic pipeline is a funnel.

Each stage reduces noise and, critically, vocabulary size. Vocabulary size matters enormously: in Bag-of-Words every unique word becomes a column. Cutting 50,000 unique words down to 12,000 shrinks the feature matrix 4×, trains faster, and overfits less.

Running example

"Satyaki was teaching NLP classes at Classroom Tech in Bengaluru, and the students weren't loving it."

Every stage on this page transforms this one sentence, step by step, until it becomes a vector of numbers a model can learn from.

03

Text normalization — the unnamed step 0

In plain words

Cleaning up — the same idea as tidying your notes before you study from them.

Make NLP and nlp the same word, open weren't into were not, and strip out web junk. But do not clean too hard: for judging a happy or angry review, !!! is useful information.

Before tokenizing, text is usually cleaned. Four operations, each with a trap attached.

Lowercasing

"NLP" and "nlp" should be one token. But beware: US (country) vs us (pronoun), Apple vs apple. Lowercasing destroys that distinction — which is exactly why NER runs before lowercasing.

Removing HTML, URLs, emojis, punctuation

Task-dependent. For sentiment analysis !!! and emoji are signal, not noise. Do not blindly strip.

Expanding contractions

weren't → were not, it's → it is. This keeps the negation visible to later stages instead of hiding it inside a clitic.

Unicode normalization

café can be encoded two different ways (é as a single codepoint, or e + combining accent). unicodedata.normalize('NFKC', text) resolves it.

1 Tokenization

In plain words

Cutting the sentence into pieces. A piece is usually a word — but not always.

don't is really two ideas, do + n't, and New York is one idea in two words. Getting the cuts right matters, because every later step works on these pieces, not on the original sentence.

What it does: splits a string into atomic units called tokens.

["Satyaki", "was", "teaching", "NLP", "classes", "at", "Classroom", "Tech",
 "in", "Bengaluru", ",", "and", "the", "students", "were", "n't", "loving", "it", "."]

Three levels of granularity

Level"unhappiness" becomesVocab sizeTrade-off
Wordunhappiness50k – 1MOut-Of-Vocabulary problem — an unseen word at test time becomes <UNK> and its information is lost
Characteru, n, h, a, p, …~100No OOV ever, but sequences get very long and meaning is diluted
Subwordun + happi + ness~30kBest of both — the sweet spot, used by every modern model

Subword tokenization — BPE, run it yourself

Byte Pair Encoding (and its cousins WordPiece and SentencePiece) is what BERT and GPT actually use. It starts from characters and greedily merges the most frequent adjacent pair, repeatedly.

lowest is now never OOV even if the model never saw that exact word — it composes from known pieces. This is also why rare names get chopped into odd fragments inside LLM tokenizers.

Separate task — sentence tokenization

Splitting text into sentences is its own hard problem, because . is ambiguous (Dr., 3.14, etc.). NLTK's punkt uses a trained statistical model, not a regex.

from nltk.tokenize import word_tokenize
word_tokenize("Dr. Das earned $3.5M. He's happy!")
# ['Dr.', 'Das', 'earned', '$', '3.5M', '.', 'He', "'s", 'happy', '!']

2 Stemming vs. Lemmatization

In plain words

Two ways to make run, runs, running and ran count as one word instead of four.

Stemming is chopping the ending off with scissors — fast, rough, and sometimes not a real word (studies → studi). Lemmatization is looking the word up in a dictionary — slower, but always a real word (ran → run).

Both collapse inflected forms to one base form, so run, runs, running, ran do not occupy four separate columns in the feature matrix. But they work in completely different ways — this is a classic interview question.

type words · compare side by side

Stemming — crude rule-based chopping

Applies suffix-stripping rules. No dictionary, no grammar, no context. A sample of the ~60 Porter Stemmer rules:

SSES    → SS     caresses   → caress
IES     → I      ponies     → poni
ATIONAL → ATE    relational → relate
ING     → ø      teaching   → teach
WordPorter stemComment
studiesstudinot a real word — acceptable, it is only a lookup key
studyingstudi✔ matches the row above
betterbetter✘ misses good
universityunivers✘ over-stemming — collides with universe
ranran✘ under-stemming — misses run

Two failure modes to name in an exam

Over-stemming — two different words collapse to the same stem, so meaning is lost. Under-stemming — two related words fail to collapse, so the relationship is missed.

Variants: Porter (mild, classic), Snowball / Porter2 (improved, multilingual), Lancaster (very aggressive, mangles words).

Lemmatization — dictionary-based and grammar-aware

Looks up the lemma — the actual dictionary headword — using a lexical database (WordNet) together with the POS tag.

WordPOS suppliedLemma
ranverbrun ✔
betteradjectivegood ✔
meetingnounmeeting
meetingverbmeet

That last pair carries the key insight: lemmatization needs POS tagging to work correctly. Without a tag, WordNet's lemmatizer defaults to noun and silently under-performs:

from nltk.stem import WordNetLemmatizer
lem = WordNetLemmatizer()
lem.lemmatize("teaching")            # 'teaching'  ← defaults to noun, wrong
lem.lemmatize("teaching", pos="v")   # 'teach'     ← correct

Choosing between them

3 Stopword removal

In plain words

the, is, at, and appear in nearly every sentence, so they say almost nothing about what a text is about. Dropping them makes the data smaller and faster.

The trap: not is on that same list. Drop it and “I did not like it” becomes “I like it” — the exact opposite.

Stopwords are high-frequency, low-information words: the, is, at, which, on, and, a, an, in. NLTK's English list holds ~179; spaCy's holds ~326.

The justification is information-theoretic: the appears in essentially every document, so its presence discriminates nothing. Removing stopwords typically cuts token count by 35–45% with no loss for topic-style tasks.

click any word to keep or drop it · watch the meaning move

When removing them is correct

Topic classification, document clustering, keyword extraction, search indexing, TF-IDF pipelines.

When it destroys your task

TaskWhy it breaks
Sentiment analysisnot is a stopword in NLTK. "I did not like it" → like — sentiment is flipped
Machine translationFunction words carry the grammar; translation is impossible without them
Question answeringwho / what / where / when are often stoplisted — the entire question disappears
Any transformer modelBERT and GPT use function words for syntax. Never strip stopwords before an LLM
Phrase search"To be or not to be" → empty string

Practical rule

Stopword lists are a starting point, not gospel. Customize them — remove not, no, never, n't for sentiment; add domain junk (patient, study in a medical corpus where every document contains them) for topic modelling.

from nltk.corpus import stopwords
sw = set(stopwords.words('english')) - {'not', 'no', 'nor', "n't", 'against'}

4 POS tagging (Part-of-Speech)

In plain words

Labelling every word with its job: noun, verb, adjective, and so on.

The same word can have two jobs — book a ticket (verb) versus read a book (noun) — so the computer has to look at the neighbouring words to decide. It cannot just look the word up.

What it does: labels each token with its grammatical category.

Satyaki/NNP was/VBD teaching/VBG NLP/NNP classes/NNS at/IN Classroom/NNP
Tech/NNP in/IN Bengaluru/NNP ,/, and/CC the/DT students/NNS were/VBD
n't/RB loving/VBG it/PRP ./.

Why it is hard — ambiguity

About 11% of English word types are ambiguous, but they happen to be the frequent ones — so roughly 40% of tokens in running text are ambiguous:

"They can fish."     can = modal verb (VB)     / fish = verb
"They can fish."     can = to put in a can (VB) / fish = noun
"He saw her duck."   duck = noun or verb?

Resolving this requires context, which is why POS tagging is a sequence labeling problem, not a per-word dictionary lookup.

How it is done, historically

  1. Rule-based (Brill tagger) — assign the most frequent tag, then apply transformation rules
  2. Statistical: HMM (Hidden Markov Model) — maximize P(tags | words) using transition probabilities P(tagi | tagi−1) and emission probabilities P(word | tag), decoded with the Viterbi algorithm. This is the textbook answer.
  3. CRF (Conditional Random Fields) — discriminative, allows rich overlapping features
  4. Neural — BiLSTM or Transformer; ~97–98% accuracy today

What POS tagging feeds: lemmatization (as shown above), parsing, NER, and feature engineering — e.g. "count of adjectives" is a strong feature for review sentiment.

5 Parsing (syntactic analysis)

In plain words

Tagging tells you each word’s job. Parsing tells you how the words are connected — who is doing what to whom.

This is what lets a machine answer “the camera is amazing but the battery is terrible” with two separate opinions about two separate things, instead of one confused average.

Parsing moves from a flat tag sequence to structure — how the words relate to one another.

8a  Constituency parsing

Groups words into nested phrases (NP = noun phrase, VP = verb phrase) using a Context-Free Grammar:

Algorithms: CYK, Earley, probabilistic CFG (PCFG). This is directly analogous to how a compiler parses source code — the same theory, applied to an ambiguous natural grammar instead of a designed one.

8b  Dependency parsing

The modern default. Builds a directed tree in which each word points to its syntactic head — no phrase nodes at all.

Key relations: nsubj (subject), dobj (direct object), amod (adjectival modifier), prep / pobj (prepositional attachment), neg (negation).

Why you would actually use it — relation extraction

"The battery life is terrible but the camera is amazing."

Bag-of-words sees terrible and amazing and shrugs. Dependency parsing yields amod(battery life, terrible) and amod(camera, amazing) — which is exactly aspect-based sentiment analysis.

The hard problem — attachment ambiguity

"I saw the man with the telescope."

→ telescope attaches to "saw"  (I used it)
→ telescope attaches to "man"  (he had it)

Both parses are grammatically valid. Resolution needs semantics or statistics — grammar alone cannot decide.

import spacy
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")
for t in nlp("Satyaki was teaching NLP classes"):
    print(t.text, t.dep_, "→", t.head.text)

6 NER — Named Entity Recognition

In plain words

Spotting the names in a text: people, companies, cities, dates, amounts of money.

Capital letters are the biggest clue, which is exactly why this has to run before we make everything lowercase. Do it in the wrong order and the clue is gone.

NER locates named things in text and classifies what kind of thing each one is. It performs two jobs at once: recognition (detecting the boundary of the entity, including multi-word spans) and classification (assigning it a type).

[Satyaki]PERSON was teaching [NLP]MISC classes at [Classroom Tech]ORG
in [Bengaluru]GPE

Breaking down the term

  • Named — the thing has a proper name (Bengaluru), not a generic description (the city)
  • Entity — a real-world object
  • Recognition — identify it and categorize it

Standard entity types

TagMeaningExample

LOCnon-political locationsHimalayas, Bay of Bengal

The BIO / IOB tagging scheme

How a per-token classifier represents multi-word spans:

TokenTag
SatyakiB-PER
wasO
atO
ClassroomB-ORG
TechI-ORG
inO
BengaluruB-GPE

B- = beginning of an entity, I- = inside, O = outside. The B/I distinction is what lets the model distinguish "Classroom Tech" (one organization) from two adjacent separate organizations.

Why NER is genuinely hard

  • Ambiguity — Washington = person, city or state? Apple = fruit or company? Jordan = person or country?
  • Nested entities — [Bank of [India]GPE]ORG
  • Novel entities — new startups and people appear daily, so a lookup list (gazetteer) can never keep up. The model must generalize from context: "… joined X as CEO" implies X is an organization.

Approaches over time: gazetteer lookup → HMM/CRF with hand-built features (capitalization, prefix/suffix, surrounding words) → BiLSTM-CRF → fine-tuned BERT (current state of the art, ~93 F1 on CoNLL-2003).

Real uses: resume parsing, anonymizing medical and legal records (PII redaction), knowledge-graph construction, financial news monitoring, and chatbot slot-filling — "book a flight to Delhi on Friday" → DEST=Delhi, DATE=Friday.

One-line answer for a viva

NER is the task of locating named entities in unstructured text and classifying them into predefined categories such as person, organization, location, date and money.

7 Vectorization — turning text into numbers

In plain words

The payoff step: real numbers at last.

Simplest: count how often each word appears. Better: give rare, telling words a bigger score than common ones. Best: give every word a short list of numbers that places it on a map, so cat lands near dog and far from democracy.

The payoff step. Three generations of methods, each one fixing the previous one's flaw.

10a  One-Hot Encoding — the baseline that fails

A vocabulary of 10,000 words means each word becomes a 10,000-dimensional vector of zeros with a single 1.

Fatal problems: the representation is enormous and sparse, and every pair of words is equidistant — cat is exactly as far from dog as it is from democracy. There is no notion of similarity at all.

10b  Bag of Words (BoW) / CountVectorizer

Represent a document as a vector of word counts. Word order is discarded — hence "bag".

Bag of Words — raw counts

Every unique word becomes a column. Every document becomes a row of counts.

Limitations

  1. Word order is lost — "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" produce identical vectors
  2. Sparsity — a 50k vocabulary with a 20-word document is 99.96% zeros
  3. No semantics — good and great are unrelated dimensions
  4. Frequent words dominate — which TF-IDF fixes

Partial fix — n-grams. Include word pairs and triples to recover local order:

CountVectorizer(ngram_range=(1,2))
# unigrams + bigrams:  "not", "good", "not good"

This rescues negation for sentiment analysis. The cost is that the vocabulary explodes combinatorially.

10c  TF-IDF (Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency)

The intuition

A word is important to a document if it appears often in that document but rarely across the corpus. the appears everywhere, so it is worthless. Bengaluru appears in 3 documents out of 10,000, so it is highly informative.

where N is the total number of documents and df(t) is the number of documents containing t. The +1 is smoothing to avoid division by zero; scikit-learn additionally L2-normalizes each row so that document length does not skew the result.

Worked example — N = 10,000 documents

TermAppears inIDF = log(N / df)Verdict
the10,000 docslog(1) = 0killed entirely
data1,000 docslog(10) ≈ 2.30moderately useful
Bengaluru10 docslog(1000) ≈ 6.91highly distinctive

So TF-IDF automatically down-weights stopwords — which is why explicit stopword removal is often redundant in a TF-IDF pipeline.

TF-IDF remains a genuinely strong baseline. For many text-classification tasks, TF-IDF + Logistic Regression lands within a few points of BERT at roughly a thousandth of the computational cost.

10d  Word Embeddings — Word2Vec and GloVe

The conceptual leap: represent each word as a dense, low-dimensional (50–300) vector of real numbers, learned so that similar words land near each other in vector space.

king   = [ 0.52, -0.18,  0.91, ...,  0.33]   ← 300 numbers
queen  = [ 0.49, -0.21,  0.88, ...,  0.31]   ← very close to king
banana = [-0.72,  0.63, -0.11, ...,  0.90]   ← far away

The distributional hypothesis (Firth, 1957)

"You shall know a word by the company it keeps." Words appearing in similar contexts have similar meanings. Every embedding method is an implementation of this single idea.

Click two words to measure cosine similarity · or run the analogy
Meaning becomes geometry. Similarity is measured by cosine of the angle between two vectors.

Word2Vec (Mikolov, Google, 2013)

A shallow neural network trained on a deliberately fake task; the output layer is thrown away and the hidden weights are kept as the embeddings. Two architectures:

PredictsBetter forSpeed
CBOWcentre word ← from contextfrequent wordsfaster
Skip-gramcontext ← from centre wordrare words, small corporaslower
Sentence: "the quick brown fox jumps"   window = 2, centre = "brown"

CBOW:      [the, quick, fox, jumps]  →  brown
Skip-gram: brown  →  [the, quick, fox, jumps]

Trained with negative sampling — instead of computing a 50,000-way softmax, the model merely discriminates the true context word from a handful of random ones. That trick is what made Word2Vec fast enough to be practical.

GloVe (Stanford, 2014)

Rather than scanning local windows, GloVe builds a global word–word co-occurrence matrix over the whole corpus and factorizes it, fitting vectors so that wiTwj + bi + bj ≈ log(Xij). Word2Vec is predictive and local; GloVe is count-based and global. In practice their performance is comparable.

The fatal limitation — embeddings are static

"I sat on the river bank" → same vector for "bank"
"I withdrew cash from the bank" → same vector for "bank"

bank gets one vector, averaging the river sense and the money sense into an incoherent blur.

FastText (Facebook) patches the OOV problem by representing a word as the sum of its character n-grams, so unhappiness composes even if it was never seen. But the polysemy problem stands.

Why this matters historically

This is precisely the gap that Transformers closed. BERT produces contextual embeddings — the vector for bank is computed from the sentence it sits in, so the two banks receive different vectors. That single change is what ended the classical pipeline era.

Pipeline ordering — and what to skip

In plain words

Some steps must come before others, or they quietly produce nonsense — no error message, just worse results.

Tag before you look words up. Find the names before you lowercase. Keep the small words until the parser has finished with them.

The order is not arbitrary. Three of these steps must run before three others, or they quietly produce garbage.

Ordering rules people get wrong

  • POS before lemmatization — otherwise the lemmatizer guesses "noun" and quietly fails
  • NER before lowercasing — capitalization is NER's strongest feature; lowercase first and you have thrown it away
  • Stopword removal after parsing — a parser needs the, of, in to build a tree
  • Lemmatize, then remove stopwords — so that inflected forms of stopwords are also caught

What to skip, by task

TaskSkip
Sentiment analysisStopword removal — or at minimum keep the negations
Translation / summarizationStopwords and stemming — you need real, complete words
Feeding a transformerEverything above. BERT and GPT want raw text; their own subword tokenizer handles it
Search indexingNothing — the full stack applies

The big picture

For LLMs this entire pipeline collapses into one step — subword tokenization — and the model learns morphology, syntax and entities implicitly from data. The classic stack still matters because it is interpretable, fast, needs no GPU, works on small data, and is the standard interview and examination material.

The whole pipeline in code

import spacy, nltk
from nltk.corpus import stopwords
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer

nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")
doc = nlp("Satyaki was teaching NLP classes at Classroom Tech in Bengaluru.")

# 1. Tokenization    4. POS tagging    2. Lemmatization
for t in doc:
    print(f"{t.text:12} {t.pos_:6} {t.lemma_:12} {t.dep_}")

# 6. NER  (before lowercasing!)
print([(e.text, e.label_) for e in doc.ents])
# [('Satyaki','PERSON'), ('NLP','ORG'), ('Classroom Tech','ORG'), ('Bengaluru','GPE')]

# 3. Stopword removal + lemmatize
sw = set(stopwords.words('english')) - {'not', 'no', "n't"}
clean = [t.lemma_.lower() for t in doc
         if not t.is_punct and t.lemma_.lower() not in sw]

# 7. Vectorization
corpus = ["I love NLP", "I love SQL", "NLP is fun fun fun"]
tfidf  = TfidfVectorizer(ngram_range=(1, 2), min_df=1)
X = tfidf.fit_transform(corpus)
print(tfidf.get_feature_names_out())
print(X.toarray().round(2))

NLTK stands for Natural Language Toolkit. It is the classic open-source Python library for NLP, created in 2001 by Steven Bird and Edward Loper at the University of Pennsylvania — originally as a teaching tool, which is why it remains the standard library in NLP courses.
